We should be more precise with our words at work. Stop shortcuts and self-soothing with the word “experiment”. It’s because we don’t know what we want to build upfront so we’re doing know what words to use.

Once you start talking about "agile values" or "agile principles", you've lost the plot. yes, it makes sense. but "agile" is an abstraction over the twelve principles/manifesto, and those in turn are an abstraction over judgement and common sense, which in turn are an abstraction over experience. Stop referring to the manifesto and start using data from your own lifetime of working in software.

"Agile" is an adjective. You can't "do" agile. Your software development life cycle can be "agile" if it values working software over comprehensive documentation, for example. But the more you say "we're doing agile software development" the more meaning the term loses. What specifically are you doing? And why is it useful?

As engineers, we should be more precise with our language at work. It's become common to use familiar terms like "experiment" or "<abbr title="Minimum Viable Product">MVP</abbr>" as a way to soothe unease when committing swathes of code, much of which is medium-quality. We allow ourselves to bypass our own processes because we're trying to learn fast. We should just say what we mean.

When I started here, Euc's technology org was refining what would be a fairly linear life cycle. It reminds me of a series of weirs: product spec flows down to design spec which flows down to engineering spec. At other times, it's like soup: all three disciplines mixed together (necessarily with a bit of salt). Both approaches seem to hold water in their own way. In the former, velocity is constrained by the work being done in series; if design or product drags their feet, we can't effectively spec our code changes. Despite this linearity, everything is subject to negotiation, all disciplines can influence the others. Documentation is created at every stage, feedback elicited, and decisions are recorded.

But we sometimes want to achieve a fast and rough feature release to collect data. The team looks more like minestrone. The problem is that product and design can craft artefacts much faster than engineering can craft code changes. In order to shorten the user feedback loop, engineering must resist the pressure to commit a lot of upfront code changes, and instead persuade the other disciplines to build in smaller increments. At first it sounds like each of these increments carries overhead, but that's the point: you're meant to course-correct at each increment based on the data.

Why does it feel like agile is so misunderstood? As long as I've been in the industry (which I'll admit is not very long) it has been associated with words like scrum and kanban. It's obvious to me now that these are distinct concepts. It can be easy, but sloppy, to pin it down as "enshittification".

While the manifesto makes intuitive sense to most readers—the ideas it presents are agreeable and well-intentioned—it should be treated as an abstraction over the authors' judgement and common sense, which in turn makes it an abstraction over their lived experience writing software.
You can't refer to the manifesto or principles in the way you might refer to a user manual.
It's not something you "do", it's the outcome of taking great care when designing your software and the processes that lead to its production.
