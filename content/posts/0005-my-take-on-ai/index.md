Since we’re talking about AI, it’s important to note that (reiterating what
others have said)

The skill ceiling is very low, and you get infinitely more value from it by
being competent in the first place

Vast majority of evangelism on social media is just that—evangelism. It always
seems to stop at “I just created several thousand lines of code” or “the time
from idea to implementation is astonishing”. If you go and dig around for blog
posts about people actually trying to use it long term on a project, you’ll find
that
[the cost of maintenance far outweighs the savings of the initial implementation](https://christophermeiklejohn.com/ai/zabriskie/reliability/2026/04/03/the-feature-that-has-never-worked.html#:~:text=AI%20agents%20can,the%20same%20function.).
So that means the skill gap is actually iteration rather than launching
something overnight.

In terms of working on iteration skills, I’ve been “practicing” by building
stuff that I don’t want to pay for. e.g. static site commenting system. It
forces me to get creative due to the constraints: how can I spend less money
than a subscription for the features I actually want. Plus it’s an excuse to see
how much I can leverage the Go standard library. Anyway thats my example

{{< /draft >}}

{{< draft >}}

Friction is the path. You have to suffer and cringe a little bit. Don’t be so
hasty to automate everything. Practice until you’re so good that the task is
boring, then automate it. The same pattern as exercising. but work motivation is
not for intrinsic benefit, it’s for external reward. Or is it? Those who enjoy
the privilege of automation have suffered long enough to master the task.

Friction is the way forward, because building good products should not feel
effortless. {{< /draft >}}
