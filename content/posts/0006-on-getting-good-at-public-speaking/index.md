I taught Les Mills classes[^programs] for three years, complete with cropped
shirts, camp dance moves, _Carly Rae_, _Fall Out Boy_, the whole deal. It's one
reason why I'm quite confident at public speaking. If you give me a lectern,
I'll give you a speech. Even so, there was a time when I _sucked_.

[^programs]: BodyBalance, BodyAttack, and BodyStep

Many people—maybe even you—assume that good presenters are just born that way.
But public speaking is not a personality trait. It's just another learnable
skill.

Unfortunately, I can't tell you how to get good. Not in a single blog post. I
can only show you where to start, by framing your problem in a new way.

Why do we mistake public speaking for a personality trait? It's due to something
I call the **shame spiral**. The shame spiral is what happens when you're new to
public speaking. You're nervous, because you're worried that you'll forget your
lines, or you think your audience doesn't want to hear what you have to say. You
think they'll get bored. Or they'll disagree. This causes you to actually forget
what you want to say, or you say it too fast, or your voice becomes monotone.
Now you've got a self-fulfilling prophecy where you _thought_ you sucked and now
you actually do suck!

The shame spiral causes new public speakers to conclude: this isn't for me, I
don't have what they have, there's something I'm missing. They give up.

The thing about public speaking is... it's complicated. You need to develop
**taste**, and you need to **practice**. You must define "good" in your own
terms, and then take the time to get "good". There's no website or book which
can replace experience—it is non-negotiable.

Watch other presenters, and watch how their audience reacts. Trust your gut and
build an opinion—are people hooked? Or are they distracted? Taste is something
you acquire after seeing a lot of good and bad public speaking.

Then practice, even if it's cringe. My camera roll is full of videos of myself
presenting to an empty room—I had built a tight iteration loop for my fitness
classes. For you it might be recording your own voice and playing it back. Find
that shame-center of your brain and turn it off.

## The shame spiral

This is a term I just made up. The shame spiral is what happens when you're new
to public speaking. You're nervous, because you're worried that you'll forget
your lines, or you think your audience doesn't want to hear what you have to
say. You think they'll get bored. Or they'll disagree. This causes you to
actually forget what you want to say, or you say it too fast, or your voice
becomes monotone. Now you've got a self-fulfilling prophecy where you _thought_
you sucked and now you actually do suck!

This only happens because the public speaking is an opaque competency. The
skills involved are not visible from the outside.

Before I get into the trademarked _5 key elements_, you'll need some context.
When teaching Les Mills, you're constrained by three things:

- Time: all moves are choreographed to music, so you've got to instruct and
  demonstrate on the beat. It's dancing and speaking at the same time (literally
  in the case of ShBam and BodyJam).
- Oxygen: you're demonstrating the exercises, which means you've got to be both
  fit enough to do the entire class _and_ speak the whole time you're doing it.
  _You got few word before get dizzy_.
- Attention: the members are there for entertainment as much as a workout. You
  _must_ engage them or they won't come back next week.

These constraints seem to act as a forcing function for growth. By getting good
at teaching BodyBalance or BodyStep, you're actually building a generalisable
public speaking skillset.

Before a class, I'd sit in my car practicing tongue twisters to improve my
elocution. In training modules, I'd practice projecting my voice (speaking, not
shouting) to a counterpart on the other side of the room. There is no affordance
for slurred speech or mumbling when on stage. Microphones are fuzzy and crackly,
and your voice must carry over blasted pop music. You will get good at
delivering complex sentences clearly and promptly.

I am told that I am a pretty good public speaker/explainer. I don’t think I
could attribute that skill to any single technique, but a few things have led me
to this point.

les mills skills which generalise: elocution (speaking clearly), speaking to a
crowd, speaking while out of breath, vocal projection, differentiation (meeting
the audience where they are, and building them up), dividing the work of
presenting into "outcomes" (5 key elements, 3 layer model), connection,
entertainment.

I've seen countless colleagues trip over their words. lose their breath. get the
jitters. at times, even I have found myself frozen or lost in my script.
Speaking to an audience (even of 1) is surprisingly unnatural to many.

One high-ROI way to improve your public speaking is to know the subject matter
really well. I don't mean to memorise a script. I mean to really get comfortable
with the domain you're speaking about, to the point where you can close your
eyes and explain a concept from that domain.

I used to film myself speaking, a lot, for a previous non-tech job. There was
also more formal training involved for that where a facilitator would provide
instant feedback on how I presented. I did that for about 2.5 years. Recording
myself, reviewing and iterating was the most important part. This was when I was
teaching group fitness at a few gyms around Sydney. It turns out that projecting
(speaking "from the belly") has a kind of commanding effect on people's
attention.

In another role, before programming, the company put me through a presentation
skills course run by
[a guy who used to be a news presenter](https://sterlingblack.com/people/simon-mariner).
I kept that instruction booklet to this day.

That also led me to try and understand why we should say certain things and
avoid saying other things. Two books come to mind: _Never Split the Difference_
and _Crucial Conversations_. Really good way to understand how to develop
interpersonal skills.

Finally, feedback from peers when they tell me I’m not being direct enough. If
you have good coworkers then this becomes a matter of practice.

Also copy whatever Derek from Veritasium is doing. Science communicators are

I have also observed others who I think are good presenters. In one case they
have a consulting background—plenty of presentation opportunities there. Another
was a chaplain, a profession in which nobody gets very far without good
communication.

But there's also one more book: _Quiet_ by Susan Cain. Ironically, she uses Tony
Robbins as an example of a "charismatic extrovert"—from the outside, one might
assume it's his larger-than-life silhouette (broad shoulders wide jaw, thick
neck), his booming deep voice, and conviction in his spoken word. However
there's much more to it than that.

All these figures share something in common: they know exactly how to get past
the rational brain and into the vegas brain (see: _Pitch Anything_) using what's
best described as "performance". It's an emotional reaction in the
audience—suspense, surprise, sadness, celebration.

However you'll never reach this vegas brain state without first _disarming_ the
audience. For example, I look out into the audience, and if there are faces I
don't recognise, then I introduce myself. I don't write this part into the
script. It's disarming, and raw, when the presenter really _sees_ the audience
this way. {{< /draft >}}
